# How the joke is built

The line:

> My smart fridge filed a restraining order after I checked it fourteen times at 3 a.m. hoping dinner had changed its mind.

Four moving parts, in the order the reader meets them:

1. **"My smart fridge"** — the setup buys personhood cheaply. A smart
   appliance is already halfway to having opinions, so nothing later needs
   explaining: the reader accepts a fridge with legal standing without
   blinking.

2. **"filed a restraining order"** — the turn. The expected beat is the
   fridge doing something technological (a notification, a low-stock
   warning). Instead it escalates straight past technology into the legal
   system, the most deadpan remedy available. The humour is in the
   disproportion: a court order, over being opened.

3. **"after I checked it fourteen times at 3 a.m."** — the confession that
   justifies the order. Two specifics do the work: *fourteen* (not "a few
   times" — a counted, ashamed number) and *3 a.m.* (an hour when nobody
   checks a fridge for a good reason). The reader recognises the behaviour
   before judging it, which is the everyday truth the judge's brief asks
   for.

4. **"hoping dinner had changed its mind"** — the closing twist, and the
   real subject of the joke. The fridge was never the problem: the narrator
   kept re-opening the door expecting the same leftovers to have become a
   different meal. "Changed its mind" hands the indecision to the food,
   which makes the narrator's hope sound almost reasonable — almost.

Economy check: 121 of the allowed 200 characters. Every clause either sets
up the turn or pays it off; there is no second joke competing with the
first, and nothing after the punchline to explain it.
